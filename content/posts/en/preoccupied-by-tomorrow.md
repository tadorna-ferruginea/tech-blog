Recently, while studying Spanish on Duolingo, I came across two fascinating words. One was *preocupada*, a derived word that can be split into the prefix *pre-* and *ocupada*. In Spanish, *ocupada* means busy. It shares an origin with the English word *occupy*: both come from the Latin *occupāre*, meaning to occupy or seize. In Spanish, then, the literal image behind being busy is that of being occupied by one’s affairs—a remarkably vivid word. Read literally, *preocupada* becomes “pre-busy”; in Spanish, it means anxious. I find this wonderfully clever because, in a sense, it reveals the nature of anxiety: being occupied by something that has not yet happened. Part of my attention and capacity for action is taken up as though I were engaged in a concrete activity, yet the event doing the occupying has not occurred. This understanding also means that we can measure the usefulness of anxiety. If this preoccupation can be discounted when the event actually occurs, offsetting the occupation it then demands, the anxiety is useful. Otherwise, we should release that occupation and allocate those resources to the life in front of us.

The second word is *mañana*, which means both morning and tomorrow. Curiously, the same polysemy can be found in languages with different origins. Returning to Chinese, we find that *míngtiān*, tomorrow, literally looks like “the sky growing bright.” I find that rather romantic. I can imagine two people sitting beneath the stars at night and talking until they are so tired that neither knows what they are saying anymore. They say goodbye reluctantly and agree to continue after daybreak. I also think of arriving at the post office just before closing to mail a letter, and of the clerks saying *mañana* to one another before leaving work. In that setting, tomorrow and morning really are the same thing. I love this word. It seems to contain both the relief of finishing a day’s work and the certainty that the sun will still rise. When I was learning to recognize the night sky a while ago, it occurred to me that the stars, as something in nature that can be understood and that appears repeatedly, may have first led people to realize that the world is regular and predictable in at least some respects. But now I think that when people created a word for tomorrow, it contained the belief that the sun would rise again. Perhaps this was an even earlier place where people found safety in regularity.

I had originally meant to go on and write about why I started learning Spanish. But the mention of regularity reminded me of the African art galleries at the Metropolitan Museum of Art. Most of the objects there are wooden sculptures. Because the hot climate is unfavorable to the preservation of wood, they are generally not very old, and their colors tend to be black or deep reddish brown, with the texture of intense oxidation. These objects have tremendous visual force. They transported me into a world governed by natural powers, where everything decays rapidly and only death is eternal. Many of the sculptures depict fierce or venomous, dangerous animals, while others attempt to portray death itself. Seen this way, the belief that the sun will rise again is not always valid. Although I hope tomorrow will still arrive, and although I often “pre-busy” myself in pursuit of the safety of predictability, perhaps I should also picture, from time to time, an enormous black wooden sculpture of a heavy truck, to remind myself to cherish a vivid and perishable life.
