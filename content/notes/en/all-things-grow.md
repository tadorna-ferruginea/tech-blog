The new semester has been underway for a week. Campus has suddenly become so lively and full of life that I am still a little unaccustomed to it. On Wednesday, I helped TA an extra astronomy lab. Professor Charls Liu is genuinely the kindest, most enthusiastic, and most gifted teacher I have ever met—there is not even room to add “one of.”

<img alt="A line of docked Citi Bikes on a New York street." class="note-image" loading="lazy" src="/images/all-things-grow-2273.webp" />

On Thursday I went to my own complex-networks class and discovered that, including me, there were only two students. So the class unfolded in a small room and in a remarkably close way. For what must have been the nth time, I was given a concise introduction to a broad family of physical phenomena—from critical phenomena to self-organization—marked by power laws. I am rather embarrassed to say that, before this, I had never formed a vivid picture of the states of matter at a critical point, or of the long-range correlations that power laws bring. Perhaps as this course unfolds, we will come to understand more concretely the crown that so many statistical-physics textbooks neglect (critical phenomena), and the beautiful trim that follows it (percolation theory). I hope I can write a few more posts about it.

The professor is Argentine; his PhD work involved sand, and he recommended Borges’s short-story collection *The Book of Sand* in class.

<img alt="People playing and resting around a temporary public installation on a Manhattan street." class="note-image" loading="lazy" src="/images/all-things-grow-2276.webp" />

In my spare time this week, I finally worked overtime and vibed the football-organizing system into existence. It has also given me a basic understanding of what happens when a browser opens a webpage: I can now think concretely about designing and deploying a system with a static frontend, a Worker backend, and a database. So naturally, the next step is to add image hosting to my blog in the same way. If I do not get too busy lately, this may be the last Note whose images and page enter the repo together.

The last thing I want to note is that I really love hiding Easter eggs. I tucked Lord Kelvin’s “two clouds” into the temperature-unit selector, and because of it learned that the line before the two clouds in Kelvin’s speech was not actually the widely circulated “the grand edifice of physics has been completed.” That may well have been a pervasive mood among physicists at the time, but many similar claims—for example, that new physics would have to be sought at the sixth decimal place—cannot be shown to have come from Kelvin. In fact, the source of the “two clouds” and several other stories about Kelvin suggest someone with a careful skepticism and an openness to new things.

My therapist, meanwhile, came to Kelvin through light and colour. She does not know the Kelvin scale, but she does know that the colour temperature of lighting is measured in kelvins.

<img alt="New York buildings seen from below, with ornamented older facades alongside a modern glass tower." class="note-image" loading="lazy" src="/images/all-things-grow-2278.webp" />

I always feel that New York is a city full of Easter eggs too. When architects of the past designed elaborate structures, patterns, colours, clocks, and fake owls to keep birds away on facades ten or more stories high, perhaps they also hoped that some passerby who happened to look up would appreciate them.
